Welcome to Life & Capital

Marco Polita · Foundational Essay

For the first time in years, I've finally decided to start a project that has been sitting in the back of my mind for a very long time.

Perhaps it was fear of judgment. Perhaps it was procrastination. Or perhaps I was simply waiting for the perfect moment.

Eventually, I realized that the perfect moment rarely arrives.

What arrives instead is the moment when you stop postponing and start building.

Tonight, in my own small way, I decided to do exactly that.

Before anything else, I think it's only fair to explain what Life & Capital is and why I decided to dedicate my time to it.

This newsletter will explore the world of Life Sciences. pharmaceuticals, biotechnology and medical innovation. through a particular lens: capital, law, business strategy and entrepreneurship.

I have always been fascinated by great business stories.

How remarkable companies are built.

How innovation is financed.

How competitive advantages are created and sustained.

How a single scientific breakthrough can evolve into a medicine that improves the lives of millions of people while simultaneously creating billions of dollars in economic value.

Life & Capital was born out of that curiosity.

A desire to better understand the intersection between science, innovation and capital.

A few words about myself. My name is Marco Polita. I am a law student with a strong passion for finance, investing and business. Over the last few years, I have become increasingly fascinated by the Life Sciences sector, which I believe is one of the most exciting frontiers of modern capitalism. an industry where scientific research, intellectual property, regulation, venture capital and entrepreneurship collide every single day.

I am not a biologist. I am not a pharmaceutical chemist. And this newsletter is not an attempt to teach science to those who understand it better than I do. Instead, it is an attempt to study and explain how the companies operating in this extraordinary sector are built, financed and scaled. I will approach that task with curiosity, intellectual honesty and a genuine desire to learn.

There is a quote by Mark Twain that has stayed with me for years: “I have never let my schooling interfere with my education.”

Not because formal education is unimportant.

Quite the opposite.

But because some of the most valuable learning happens outside the classroom—through curiosity, reading, conversations and the willingness to continuously explore new ideas.

This newsletter represents exactly that for me.

A public learning journey.

To everyone who chooses to spend their time reading these articles, I offer my commitment.

I do not promise to always be right.

I do promise to remain curious, thoughtful and willing to improve over time.

If you decide to join me on this journey, I look forward to sharing what I discover along the way.

Thank you for reading the first issue of Life & Capital.

Marco

Why Biotech Venture Capital Invests in Drugs That Have a 90% Chance of Failing

Marco Polita · Deep Dive

There is one number that, on its own, seems to make the entire biotech industry look irrational: roughly 90% of drugs that enter clinical development will never reach the market.

They do not fail because investors are naive. They do not fail because scientists are incompetent. They fail because human biology is one of the most complex, opaque, and expensive systems capital has ever tried to decode.

And yet, every year, billions of dollars continue to flow into biotech. Venture capital funds, crossover investors, corporate venture arms of large pharmaceutical companies, and specialized funds finance startups that often have no revenue, no approved products, and sometimes not even clinical data in humans.

So the question is not: why invest in something that fails almost all the time?

The right question is: what is capital actually buying when it invests in biotech?

The short answer is this: biotech VC does not buy drugs. It buys experiments capable of reducing uncertainty.

The Number That Scares Everyone

According to the report by BIO, Informa Pharma Intelligence, and QLS Advisors, based on 9,704 clinical programs between 2011 and 2020, the probability that a drug candidate entering Phase I will eventually receive approval is 7.9%.

In other words: more than 9 out of 10 drugs that enter clinical trials never make it to the finish line.

The weakest point is Phase II, the stage where a company must show that the drug is not only tolerable, but that it produces a real efficacy signal in patients. Only 28.9% of programs move from Phase II to the next stage. Phase I has a transition success rate of 52%, Phase III of 57.8%, while once a drug reaches regulatory submission, the probability of approval rises to 90.6%.

The full journey is long: on average, it takes 10.5 years to move from Phase I to approval.

This is the first thing to understand: in biotech, failure is not an accident. It is the normal cost of information.

Every clinical trial is a question asked of nature. Is the molecule safe? Is the biological target relevant? Does the biomarker truly predict response? Is the observed effect strong enough to convince regulators, physicians, and payers?

Capital funds these questions. And it gets rewarded when a positive answer turns a scientific hypothesis into a financial asset.

Biotech Is Not a Lottery. It Is a Portfolio of Options

From the outside, a biotech startup looks like a binary bet: either the drug works, or it goes to zero.

From the inside, it looks more like a chain of options.

A fund invests today to bring an asset to the next information event: a preclinical result, an IND filing, a Phase I readout, a Phase II proof-of-concept, a validated biomarker, a partnership, an acquisition.

Value does not come only from final approval. It comes from the fact that, after each successful milestone, uncertainty decreases and the asset becomes understandable to someone else: a late-stage fund, the public market, or a large pharmaceutical company.

This is where the financial logic becomes interesting.

A drug in Phase I may have only a 7.9% statistical probability of reaching the market. But if it is relatively inexpensive to bring it to the next clinical readout, and if a positive result can multiply its value, then the investment may make sense.

VC is not necessarily saying: “this drug will be approved.”

It is saying: “the next experiment can radically change the price of this risk.”

Why Big Pharma Buys the Risk That VC Has Refined

Large pharmaceutical companies have a structural problem: patents expire.

Every blockbuster has an economic death date. When exclusivity ends, generics or biosimilars arrive, prices fall, and margins evaporate. To stay alive, Big Pharma must constantly refill its pipeline.

But developing drugs internally has become increasingly expensive. Deloitte estimates that in 2024 the average development cost per asset among leading biopharma companies reached around $2.23 billion. The expected average return on R&D improved, but remains fragile: 5.9% in 2024, according to Deloitte.

This pressure makes external biotech an essential part of the industrial engine.

Startups absorb early risk. VC funds finance scientific hypotheses that are still too small, too fragile, or too uncertain to move the needle for a multinational pharmaceutical company. But when a biotech company produces convincing data, the asset changes category. It moves from experiment to strategic option.

At that point, Big Pharma can pay.

Not because it loves risk. But because it prefers to buy risk that has already been partially de-risked, with clinical data, intellectual property, and a clearer regulatory rationale.

Biotech VC is therefore an intermediary in the risk chain: it takes raw scientific uncertainty and tries to transform it into uncertainty that can be financially priced.

Not All Risks Are Equal

Saying that “90% of drugs fail” is true, but incomplete.

The probability of success changes dramatically depending on the therapeutic area, modality, clinical design, and the ability to select the right patients.

Rare disease programs, for example, have a likelihood of approval of 17%, more than twice the overall average. Programs that use biomarkers to select patients reach a probability of approval of 15.9%. In oncology, the average is lower, around 5.3%, but immuno-oncology rises to 12.4%.

This changes everything.

Biotech is not a single market. It is a collection of scientific, regulatory, and financial micro-markets. A drug for a rare disease with high unmet need, small trials, clear endpoints, and robust biomarkers does not have the same risk profile as a drug for a high-prevalence chronic disease, where studies are enormous, long, and expensive.

This is why the best biotech investors are not simply investors. They are interpreters of biology, regulation, and market structure.

They must ask: is the target validated? Is the trial well designed? Will the clinical signal be readable? Is the endpoint acceptable to the FDA? Is the market large enough? Will payers reimburse it? Is there a strategic buyer?

In biotech, risk should not be avoided. It should be selected.

The Myth of the Biotech Power Law

In technology venture capital, we are used to the idea that a small number of winners generate almost all the returns. An investment in a major software platform can return 100 times the capital. Many funds accept dozens of failures because they are searching for that one enormous outlier.

In biotech, the dynamic is different.

According to an analysis by Bay Bridge Bio, excluding the exceptional impact of Moderna and BioNTech during Covid, 13% of biotech VC deals generate 43% of returns. That is still a concentrated distribution, but less extreme than in software venture capital.

This means biotech VC cannot survive only by waiting for a 100x miracle. It needs more discipline.

It needs to buy well. It needs to avoid overpaying for scientific excitement. It needs to build portfolios where some assets can generate acquisitions, licensing deals, or IPOs even before final approval.

Biotech is venture capital, but with different mathematics: more milestones, more staged capital, more intermediate value, more dependence on public markets and Big Pharma.

The Real Product Is Information

The most counterintuitive point is this: often, a biotech company is not selling a drug. It is selling information.

A positive clinical trial is not only a step toward the patient. It is a change in how the market values a hypothesis.

Before the data, value is narrative: a biological theory, a credible team, intellectual property, perhaps promising animal models. After the data, if the signal is strong, value becomes negotiable.

The startup can raise a larger round. It can go public. It can sign a partnership with upfront payments and future milestones. It can be acquired.

In this sense, biotech capital funds a sequence of conversions:

from hypothesis to molecule;
from molecule to clinical candidate;
from clinical candidate to signal in patients;
from signal in patients to strategic asset;
from strategic asset to commercial drug.

Each step can destroy value. But each step, if successful, can multiply it.

Why Investors and Funds Keep Doing It

There are at least five reasons.

The first is asymmetry. A single successful drug can generate billions of dollars in annual sales. Even if the probability is low, the payoff can be enormous.

The second is the possibility of exiting earlier. VC does not necessarily have to wait ten years and hold until final approval. It can sell after a Phase II readout, a partnership, or a public listing.

The third is specialization. In biotech, knowing how to interpret a clinical endpoint, a biomarker, or a mechanism of action can create a real informational advantage over generalist capital.

The fourth is structural demand from Big Pharma. Large pharmaceutical companies need pipelines and often prefer to buy them externally once part of the risk has been absorbed by others.

The fifth is potential social value. When biotech works, it does not only generate financial returns. It produces vaccines, cell therapies, drugs for rare diseases, oncology treatments, and new biological modalities. This does not automatically make every investment good, but it explains why capital remains attracted to the sector.

Where It Is Easy to Misread the Story

The first trap is thinking that a low probability of success automatically makes an investment bad. It does not. A risky investment can be excellent if the price is right and the payoff is large enough.

The second trap is confusing science with finance. A drug can be scientifically elegant but commercially weak. It can work, but not enough. It can have promising data, but a market that is too small or too crowded. It can be approved, but not reimbursed well.

The third trap is forgetting timing. In biotech, the moment of exit matters almost as much as the clinical result. Selling after a strong Phase II can be more rational than funding a massive and risky Phase III.

The fourth trap is underestimating the cost of capital. When interest rates rise and IPO markets close, even the best science can get stuck without financing.

The fifth trap is thinking that Big Pharma only buys innovation. It also buys time, protection from the patent cliff, competitive positioning, and strategic optionality.

The Financial Moral of Biotech

Biotech VC looks irrational only if we see it as a bet on final approval.

But the real game is different.

Capital enters when uncertainty is at its highest and tries to transform it into information. Not all information is useful. Not all positive data creates value. Not every biological target becomes a drug. But when the chain works, an experiment worth a few million dollars can become an asset worth billions.

Biotech Venture Capital invests in drugs that have a 90% chance of failing because it is not buying certainty.

It is buying the right to discover something before everyone else.

And in a sector where a discovery can change the trajectory of a disease, a company, and a portfolio, that right can be worth a lot.

The point is not that biotech beats probability.

The point is that it tries to price it better.

What This Means For Investors

If biotech is viewed only as a collection of drug approvals and failures, the sector appears irrational.

If it is viewed as a market that continuously prices scientific uncertainty, the behavior of investors becomes easier to understand.

The question is not whether most drugs fail.

The question is whether the market is correctly pricing the information generated along the way.
